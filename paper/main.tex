\title{Queue Shift: Updating Classifiers Under a Staffing Budget}
\author{Gaurav Sood}
\date{August 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When a classifier routes cases to specialized queues, a model update changes both prediction
accuracy and the distribution of work. Backward-compatibility methods count negative flips, which
do not identify how much work must move between queues. I define queue shift as half the $L_1$
distance between incumbent and proposed queue totals, and choose the batch assignment that
maximizes candidate-model probability subject to budgets on queue shift and on expected negative
flips. Without the flip budget, or with a flip penalty, the problem is a minimum-cost flow with an
integral optimum; with a hard flip budget it is a small integer program. At the queue shift and
expected flips of any comparison assignment, including any point on a negative-flip-rate
interpolation path, the solution has at least the comparison's expected accuracy under the
candidate's probabilities. The flow optimum is also a per-queue price rule, which links the method
to online allocation and gives the natural low-cost alternative. Simulations then map where the
constraint matters. Holding queue totals fixed costs little when an update mostly reorders cases
and a great deal when it moves real volume between queues. Learned prices nearly match exact
assignment in accuracy but miss the movement budget in most batches.
\end{abstract}

\section{The operational problem}

Suppose a support organization sends predicted billing tickets to a billing team, technical
tickets to a technical team, and so on. It replaces its routing classifier after a new feature or
model improves accuracy on later held-out data. The raw update may also change the number of cases
sent to each team. Even when total demand is fixed, managers may need to move capacity across
queues.

This creates a decision after prediction. The organization has already chosen a better candidate
model. It now needs to decide which queue receives each case, balancing the candidate's accuracy
signal against a limit on workload redistribution. Retraining the model to reduce negative flips
addresses a related concern, but it does not directly control queue totals.

Research on prediction churn and backward-compatible updates measures changes to individual
decisions, including cases on which a formerly correct prediction becomes wrong
\citep{fard2016launch,bansal2019updates,srivastava2020empirical,traeuble2021backward,jiang2022churn}.
That protection can matter when people depend on particular decisions. The staffing problem is
different because cases can trade queues while every team receives the same amount of work.

Let $i=1,\ldots,n$ index cases and $k=1,\ldots,K$ index queues. The incumbent sends case $i$ to
$a_i$, producing load
\[
  m_k=\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}\{a_i=k\}.
\]
For a proposed assignment $z$, let $n_k(z)$ be its load in queue $k$. Define
\[
  \shift(z;m)
  =\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^K |n_k(z)-m_k|
  =\sum_{k=1}^K [n_k(z)-m_k]_+.
\]
The equality follows because old and new loads both sum to $n$. Queue shift is the smallest number
of workload units that must cross queue boundaries to reconcile the two load vectors. A value of
zero preserves every queue total, even if individual cases exchange places.

That distinction is why negative-flip rate (NFR) does not identify the staffing cost. Consider
three cases with true labels $(1,2,1)$ and incumbent assignments $(1,1,2)$. Compare proposed
assignments $(2,2,2)$ and $(2,1,1)$. Each proposal has one correct prediction, one negative flip,
one positive flip, and two changed case labels. The first changes queue loads from $(2,1)$ to
$(0,3)$, so its queue shift is two. The second keeps loads at $(2,1)$, so its queue shift is zero.
Accuracy, NFR, and total label churn are identical, but the staffing implications differ.

Counting cases treats a billing ticket and a fraud investigation as the same amount of work.
Staffing cares about hours or money. Let $c_k\geq 0$ be the price of one case in queue $k$: one for
a case count, average handle time for hours, average cost per case for money. The cases are the
currency and $c_k$ is the exchange rate. The workload shift
\[
  \shift_c(z;m)=\sum_{k=1}^K c_k\,[n_k(z)-m_k]_+
\]
prices the work added to queues beyond their incumbent loads. Work removed from one queue earns no
credit against work added to another, because staff are not interchangeable across queues. With
$c_k=1$, $\shift_c=\shift$. Per-queue averages ignore variation in handling time within a queue
and differences in skill; the model keeps predicted class as the unit that determines a staffed
queue.

\section{Assignment under movement and flip budgets}

Let $p_{ik}$ be the candidate model's probability that case $i$ belongs in queue $k$. For any
assignment $z$, define its average candidate score as
\[
  \valuefn_p(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n p_{i,z_i}.
\]
Queue totals are not the only thing an update disturbs. Each case $i$ that leaves its incumbent
queue $a_i$ is a changed decision, and it is a negative flip exactly when its true class is $a_i$.
Give each case a nonnegative weight $w_i$ and define the flip load
\[
  F_w(z)=\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\,\mathbf{1}\{z_i\neq a_i\}.
\]
Two weights matter. With $w_i=1$, $F_w$ counts changed labels. With $w_i=p_{i,a_i}$, $F_w$ is the
expected number of negative flips under the candidate's probabilities, because a moved case is a
negative flip with probability $p_{i,a_i}$. Neither weight uses labels. Given an integer movement
budget $B$ and a flip budget $C$, Queue Shift solves
\begin{equation}
  z^*(B,C)\in\arg\max_z \valuefn_p(z)
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad \shift(z;m)\leq B,\quad F_w(z)\leq C.
  \label{eq:assignment}
\end{equation}
Setting $C=\infty$ gives the movement-only problem. The dominance result and error bounds
below hold with the priced workload shift $\shift_c$ in place of $\shift$; the queue-price result
needs the flow form. Equal prices keep that form, with the overflow
capacity $B/c$; unequal prices turn the movement budget into a weighted side constraint, solved,
like the flip budget, as an exact integer program.

The movement-only problem is a minimum-cost flow. A source sends one unit through each case node.
Every case connects to every queue with cost $1-p_{ik}$. Queue $k$ sends up to $m_k$ units directly
to the sink at no movement cost. Additional units pass through one shared overflow node, whose edge
to the sink has capacity $B$. Integer supplies and capacities imply an integral optimum, so the
linear program assigns every case to exactly one queue. A flip penalty keeps this structure:
maximizing $\valuefn_p(z)-\lambda F_w(z)/n$ only adds $\lambda w_i$ to the cost of every arc from
case $i$ to a queue other than $a_i$. A hard flip budget does not. It adds one constraint that
couples all cases, the linear relaxation can be fractional, and the problem must be solved as an
integer program. With HiGHS at a zero optimality gap, a 300-case, 15-queue batch takes well under
a second and a 2,000-case batch a few seconds; the penalty form solves the same 2,000-case batch in
under a tenth of a second. The two forms agree where they meet. A penalty solution $z_\lambda$ is
optimal for the hard problem at $C=F_w(z_\lambda)$, since any assignment with no more flip load and
a higher score would also have a higher penalized objective.

The transport construction itself is established. Probability-based label allocation and
classification under capacity constraints already use transport or mathematical programming
\citep{tai2021sinkhorn,rabkin2024resource}, and predict-then-optimize methods incorporate
downstream decisions into predictive systems \citep{elmachtoub2022smart}. Work on prediction churn
asks which updates change individual decisions \citep{traeuble2021backward,watsondaniels2024churn}.
The contribution here is the incumbent-relative pair of constraints, one on queue totals and one
on individual decisions, and the like-for-like comparison it permits with compatibility rules.

\subsection{A matched-budget guarantee}

NFR interpolation forms probabilities
\[
  p^{\alpha}_{ik}=(1-\alpha)p^0_{ik}+\alpha p^1_{ik},
\]
where $p^0$ and $p^1$ are incumbent and candidate probabilities. Its hard assignment $b^\alpha$
traces a path from the incumbent to the raw candidate as $\alpha$ moves from zero to one. A
deployment team can select a point that meets an NFR target while retaining validation accuracy.
Queue Shift takes that point's movement and expected negative flips as its budgets.

\begin{proposition}[Dominance at matched movement and flips]
For any baseline assignment $b$ and weights $w$, set $B=\shift(b;m)$ and $C=F_w(b)$. Then
$z^*=z^*(B,C)$ satisfies
\[
  \valuefn_p(z^*)\geq \valuefn_p(b),\qquad \shift(z^*;m)\leq \shift(b;m),\qquad
  F_w(z^*)\leq F_w(b).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The baseline is feasible for Equation~\ref{eq:assignment} by the definition of $B$ and $C$. An
optimum cannot have a lower objective value than a feasible comparison assignment, and every
feasible assignment satisfies both budgets.
\end{proof}

The proof is one line; the content is in what is matched. With $w_i=p_{i,a_i}$, Queue Shift moves
no more work between queues, risks no more expected negative flips, and has no lower expected
accuracy than the baseline, all under the candidate's probabilities. Matching movement alone, the
special case $C=\infty$, drops the protection an NFR rule exists to provide. In the simulations
below, the movement-only solution keeps at least \QSZeroNFRRawShareMin\% of the raw candidate's
negative-flip rate even at zero movement. A comparison at matched movement alone is therefore not
like for like.

If $p_{ik}$ is the true conditional probability of class $k$, $\valuefn_p(z)$ is conditional
expected accuracy and $F_w$ with $w_i=p_{i,a_i}$ is the conditional expected number of negative
flips. Estimated probabilities require more care. Let $q_{ik}$ denote the true conditional
probabilities, $e_{ik}=q_{ik}-p_{ik}$, and $D=\{i:z^*_i\neq b_i\}$. Cases outside $D$ contribute
the same error to both assignments, so
\begin{equation}
  \valuefn_q(z^*)-\valuefn_q(b)
  \geq \valuefn_p(z^*)-\valuefn_p(b)
  -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in D}
  \left(|e_{i,z_i^*}|+|e_{i,b_i}|\right).
  \label{eq:error-bound}
\end{equation}
The same argument bounds the flip budget's error. Each moved case $i$ contributes $q_{i,a_i}$
rather than $p_{i,a_i}$ to the true expected flip load, so true expected negative flips exceed
planned ones by at most $\sum_{i:z^*_i\neq a_i}|e_{i,a_i}|$. Neither bound can be computed in
deployment, because $q$ is unobserved, and both are loose in our simulations. Their use is
diagnostic. Errors matter only on the cases the method moves, so the calibration worth checking
is calibration on those cases. Replaying the solver on labeled history measures it directly.

\subsection{Queue prices}

The flow form has a second reading that matters for practice.

\begin{proposition}[Queue prices]
For any $B$ and penalty $\lambda\ge 0$, there are prices $u_1,\ldots,u_K$ such that the
penalized flow optimum assigns every case to a queue that maximizes
\[
  p_{ik}-\lambda w_i\mathbf{1}\{k\neq a_i\}-u_k.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The prices are the optimal dual values (node potentials) of the queue nodes. Complementary
slackness for minimum-cost flow requires every arc carrying flow to have zero reduced cost and
every empty arc nonnegative reduced cost. For the arcs leaving case $i$, this is the stated
maximization.
\end{proof}

The optimum is thus a per-queue threshold rule: subtract a price from each queue's probability and
take the best queue. The price falls on probabilities, not logits. Ties among the maximizing queues
occur only for marginal cases; in our checks they never involve more than one case per queue. The
solver returns these prices.

Two consequences follow. First, prices learned on history and then applied case by case are the
natural low-cost competitor. This is the primal-dual approach to online allocation, which learns
dual prices on early arrivals and uses them on later ones \citep{devanur2009adwords,agrawal2014online},
and it is how a routing system that cannot wait for a batch would deploy Queue Shift. Second,
learned prices carry no guarantee for a given batch. Prices fitted to one mix of cases move the
wrong amount of work when the next batch has a different mix. Section~\ref{sec:regimes} measures
how much this matters.

\section{When the constraint binds}
\label{sec:regimes}

The propositions hold for any scores. Whether they matter depends on how far the candidate's
predicted queue mix is from the incumbent's, how much of that difference the organization can
absorb, and whether assignments can wait for a batch. This section names the settings where these
conditions arise and uses simulation to show how large the effects can be. The simulations
illustrate mechanisms; they do not estimate magnitudes for any organization.

\paragraph{Where queue totals are fixed in the short run.}
Queue Shift is useful only when work routed to a queue must be handled by staff assigned to that
queue over the planning horizon. That describes support desks staffed by skill or product, content
moderation queues organized by policy area or language, insurance claims and fraud review tiers,
and compliance alert review. In each, rosters are set days ahead and cross-training is partial. It
does not describe a pool of interchangeable agents, where only total volume matters. Assignment
must also be able to wait for a batch, such as an overnight backlog or a daily review queue. Live
routing of one case at a time can use only the price rule of Proposition~2.

\paragraph{Mechanisms that move volume.}
Four scenarios make the candidate's predicted mix differ from the incumbent's in different ways.
\emph{Stable}: five queues with unequal base rates; the candidate adds an independent signal that
sharpens every class. \emph{Confused pair}: the incumbent barely separates the two largest queues,
as with billing and technical tickets, and the candidate's new feature separates them; volume moves
between those queues in a consistent direction. \emph{Stale priors}: demand changed after the
incumbent was trained, as after a product launch, and the candidate was trained on recent data;
here the candidate's mix is closer to the truth, and holding the incumbent's totals preserves an
outdated plan; the budget then limits how much of the needed change happens before staffing
catches up. \emph{Specialist
queues}: fifteen queues with Zipf-distributed base rates, where small queues have little slack.
Each scenario crosses four batch regimes: 2,000 or 300 cases with a fixed class mix, and 300 or 100
cases whose class mix is redrawn for every batch from a Dirichlet distribution around the base
rates. Every scenario uses 30 independently trained pairs of multinomial logistic models, released
only after the candidate wins on an independent 2,000-case sample. Raw candidate queue shift ranges
from \RegimeRawShiftMin\% to \RegimeRawShiftMax\% of cases.

\paragraph{Matching flips changes the comparison.}
Figure~\ref{fig:regimes-frontier} shows every method in the 300-case fixed-mix regime. Matched on
queue shift alone (blue), Queue Shift appears to dominate interpolation by a wide margin at small
budgets. Its negative-flip rate shows why. At zero movement, where interpolation keeps the
incumbent and has no flips, movement-only Queue Shift flips \QSZeroNFRMin--\QSZeroNFRMax\% of the
cases the incumbent got right, at least \QSZeroNFRRawShareMin\% of the raw candidate's rate. Matched
on both queue shift and expected negative flips (green), the realized negative-flip rate stays
within \JointNFRGapMax{} points of interpolation's, and the true accuracy gain over interpolation is
\JointQuarterGainMin--\JointQuarterGainMax{} points at $\alpha=0.25$ and
\JointHalfGainMin--\JointHalfGainMax{} points at $\alpha=0.5$ across scenarios and batch regimes. At
$\alpha=0$ the flip budget is zero and both methods keep the incumbent. This like-for-like gain is
smaller than the movement-only gain, and it is the relevant one when an NFR target is binding.

\paragraph{What holding queue totals costs.}
Table~\ref{tab:lock-cost} reports the accuracy lost by holding every queue total fixed, relative
to deploying the raw candidate. The cost depends on the mechanism, not on the size of the update.
When an update mostly reorders cases within a stable mix (the stable and specialist scenarios with
fixed-mix batches), zero movement costs \LockReorderMin--\LockReorderMax{} points and keeps at least
\LockReorderKeptMin\% of the update. When the update legitimately moves volume (confused pair and
stale priors), it costs \LockVolumeMin--\LockVolumeMax{} points and keeps only
\LockVolumeKeptMin--\LockVolumeKeptMax\%. Small, volatile batches raise the cost even for
reordering updates, to \LockSmallMin--\LockSmallMax{} points, because the incumbent's totals on a
small batch track noise as well as demand. The constraint is nearly free in the first case and a
real tradeoff in the second, which is where a nonzero budget, chosen with the staffing plan, earns
its keep. An earlier hypothesis that the matched-budget gain over interpolation grows with the raw
candidate's queue shift is not supported (Figure~\ref{fig:mix-gap}); the mechanism matters
more than the size of the shift.

\paragraph{Prices versus exact assignment.}
Proposition~2 suggests a cheaper rule: learn queue prices once on history and apply them case by
case. Table~\ref{tab:offsets} compares this rule with exact per-batch assignment at interpolation's
$\alpha=0.5$ budget. The prices are close to optimal for the movement they produce, falling short
of the exact assignment at that movement by at most \OffsetsSuboptMax{} points anywhere in the
design. But they do not control movement. At $\alpha=0.5$ they exceed the batch's budget in
\OffsetsOverBudgetHalfMin--\OffsetsOverBudgetHalfMax\% of batches, and at zero movement they move
\OffsetsOverZeroMin--\OffsetsOverZeroMax\% of cases. The overshoot is largest for small, volatile
batches and many small queues. The practical division is therefore clear. Where a budget must hold
in every batch, solve each batch exactly. Where routing is online or the budget is a soft average,
prices from Queue Shift are a sound approximation, and the exact solver supplies them.

\section{What a deployment would validate}

A practical rollout separates model release from batch assignment. First, compare the raw
candidate with the incumbent on later labeled data and reject candidates that do not improve the
chosen predictive criterion. Second, measure the raw candidate's queue shift and the cost of
holding queue totals on replayed history; if both are small, the constraint is nearly free and
the budget can be tight. Third, choose the two budgets: queue movement from the staffing plan, and
expected negative flips from an existing NFR target or the interpolation point that meets it.
Fourth, solve Equation~\ref{eq:assignment} on each planning batch. Finally, monitor queue loads,
which hold by construction, and evaluate accuracy and negative flips when labels arrive.

The matched-budget guarantee needs no labels at assignment time, but its accuracy and flip
interpretation needs useful probability scores on the cases the solver moves. Reliability plots
restricted to moved cases, proper scoring rules, and out-of-time replay can check that condition.

Three limitations matter before claims become broad. First, workload is priced by queue averages;
case-specific handling times and skill constraints within a queue are outside the model. Second, the
evidence is synthetic, with correctly specified logistic models except where scores are rescaled.
Third, the comparison is with one compatibility rule. A field study should report service times,
queue-specific capacity, forecast horizon, and realized staffing actions, and should compare with
the compatibility method the organization actually uses.

\section{Conclusion}

For routing classifiers, compatibility is partly an allocation problem. Negative flips protect
individual decisions; queue shift protects the staffing plan. Queue Shift optimizes within both
and, at the same movement and expected flips as any comparison assignment, has at least its
expected accuracy under the candidate's probabilities. The flow optimum is a price rule, which
gives online routing a principled approximation. The simulations locate where this matters. When
an update reorders cases, holding queue totals is nearly free; when it moves volume, holding them
is costly and the budget should be set together with staffing. The result is modest, exact, and
testable.
